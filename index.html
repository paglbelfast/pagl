Who we are

About PAGL

PAGL stands for Protestants Against Gaelic Language. The group has expressed concerns about compulsory Irish-language policies, language-related public spending and the treatment of people who do not speak Irish. This site brings together those views for public examination and discussion.

Our coverage focuses primarily on the Republic of Ireland, while also addressing policy developments in Northern Ireland. Views expressed in PAGL commentary should not be taken as representative of all Protestants or all non-Irish speakers.

PAGL perspectives

Articles and arguments

The articles below adapt themes from material supplied for the PAGL website. They present questions and viewpoints, not independently verified findings of discrimination or waste.

Education · Opinion

Should Irish-language study be compulsory?

PAGL questions the extent to which Irish should be a compulsory part of education. Its argument centres on parental choice, students' interests and the time allocated to different subjects. Supporters of compulsory Irish regard it as an important means of sustaining a national language and cultural heritage. Any assessment should consider curriculum rules, exemptions, educational outcomes and the views of students and teachers.

Employment · Equality

Language requirements and public-sector jobs

A recurring PAGL concern is that Irish-language requirements could exclude otherwise qualified applicants, including Protestants and people who do not speak Irish. Whether a particular requirement is proportionate depends on the duties of the job and applicable equality law. Historical allegations about political patronage should be examined against archival evidence rather than assumed to apply today.

Taxpayers · Public services

How should Irish-language spending be assessed?

Public discussion of language policy often includes spending on bilingual signs, public transport information, the national water utility’s communications, translation, Irish-language television and Irish-medium education. The useful questions are what each service costs, what legal duties apply, who benefits and what alternatives exist. Costs should be separated by agency and financial year; no overall figure is asserted here without a supporting budget.

History · Debate

The Language Freedom Movement and historical opposition

Opposition to compulsory Irish has a history in the Republic of Ireland, including campaigns associated with the Language Freedom Movement. PAGL's leaflet refers to historical political figures and arguments about language qualifications for state employment. These accounts warrant careful checking against contemporary speeches, archives and academic histories.

Culture · Literature

Ireland's English-language literary heritage

Writers associated with Ireland's English-language literary tradition include Jonathan Swift, Oscar Wilde, W. B. Yeats, George Bernard Shaw, Sean O'Casey and James Joyce. Their work forms part of Ireland's cultural history alongside its Irish-language literary heritage. The place of both traditions is an important subject for historical discussion.

Public policy · Commentary

Street signs, language services and political symbolism

Posts supplied from a PAGL-related X account argue that public signs should prioritise clear information and question whether language policy is sometimes used to express political identity. Others see bilingual signs as recognition of Irish as a living language. Specific signage decisions can be assessed through the applicable legislation, public consultations, accessibility needs and published costs.

Editorial note: The original leaflet contains numerical and historical assertions, including an “87%” figure and a claim about public-service jobs, that have not been substantiated here. They are deliberately not presented as established facts. Quotations and commentary from social media should be attributed to their original authors.

Drumcree · Background

Parading rights, residents' concerns and the Garvaghy Road

Drumcree remains a significant issue in discussions of Orange Order traditions, freedom of assembly, local residents' rights and policing. Unionist participants emphasise the cultural and religious importance of the parade; Garvaghy Road residents have objected to the route and raised concerns about community consent. Official determinations and the details of each year's events should be checked before drawing conclusions.

Historical archive

Election literature and historical context

A 1938 election advertisement from County Meath used the slogan “Meath Wants No More Migrants”. It referred to internal migration associated with the Rathcairn Irish-speaking settlement. The wording reflects a historical political campaign, not PAGL's position on people moving between communities today.
